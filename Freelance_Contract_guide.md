# Freelancer's Beginner Guide: Creating a Client Contract

> **Note:** This is a coaching guide, not legal advice. For big projects or large amounts of money, get a lawyer to review your template once. After that, you can reuse it for years.

---

## Why a Contract Matters

A contract is not about distrust. It's about **clarity**. Most client problems (late payments, endless changes, "I thought you'd do that too") happen because things were never written down. A good contract protects both of you.

---

## Step 1: Talk First, Write Later

Before you write anything, have a clear conversation with the client about:

- What exactly will you deliver?
- When is the deadline?
- How much, and how will they pay?
- How many changes are included?

Write the contract only after you both agree verbally. The contract just records what you already agreed on, so there are no surprises.

---

## Step 2: Put These Sections in Your Contract

### 1. Parties Involved
Your full name (or business name) and the client's full name or company name, with addresses and contact details. Be exact. A vague name causes problems later.

### 2. Scope of Work
This is the **most important** section. Describe exactly what you will do, and also what you will *not* do.

- **Weak:** "Design a website."
- **Strong:** "Design a 5-page website (Home, About, Services, Blog, Contact) in Figma. Development and hosting are not included."

The more specific you are, the less you'll fight about it later.

### 3. Deliverables and Timeline
List what you'll hand over and the dates. Break big projects into milestones, for example: draft by 10th, revisions by 15th, final by 20th.

Also add that deadlines depend on the client giving feedback and materials on time. If they delay, your deadline shifts too.

### 4. Payment Terms
Clearly state:

- Total fee
- Currency
- Advance amount (suggested **30-50% upfront** for beginners)
- Milestone or final payment dates
- Payment method (bank transfer, UPI, PayPal, etc.)
- Due date for invoices (for example, within 7 days)
- **Late fee**, for example 2% per week after the due date

> Never start work without the advance. This one rule will save you a lot of stress.

### 5. Revisions
Say how many rounds of changes are included, say 2. Anything beyond that is charged extra, at an hourly rate or a fixed amount. Without this clause, clients can ask for changes forever.

### 6. Change Requests
If the client wants something outside the original scope, it needs a written agreement on extra cost and time. This protects you from "scope creep," where small additions slowly turn into double the work.

### 7. Client Responsibilities
What you need from them: content, logos, login access, feedback within X days, and so on. This makes it their job to keep things moving.

### 8. Ownership and Rights (Intellectual Property)
Who owns the final work, and when? The common approach: ownership transfers to the client **only after full payment**. Also mention whether you can show the work in your portfolio. Most freelancers include this.

### 9. Confidentiality
If you'll see private business info, promise to keep it private. Clients like seeing this.

### 10. Termination (Cancellation)
What happens if either side wants to stop?

- How much notice is needed (for example, 7 days written notice)
- The client pays for all work done until that point
- The advance is non-refundable (or partly refundable, your choice)

### 11. Liability
Keep it simple: your responsibility is limited to the amount the client paid you. This protects you if something goes wrong.

### 12. Dispute Handling and Governing Law
Say which country or state's law applies, and how disputes will be handled (talk first, then mediation, then court). Beginners often skip this, but it matters.

### 13. Signatures and Date
Both sides sign and date. An e-signature is fine in most places.

---

## Step 3: Write in Plain Language

Don't copy-paste heavy legal jargon you don't understand. If you can't explain a line to your friend, rewrite it. A contract the client can easily understand gets signed faster, and it holds up fine.

---

## Step 4: Use a Template, Then Customize

Don't start from a blank page every time. Create one solid master template. Then for each client, only change the names, scope, price, and dates.

You can find free freelance contract templates online (Bonsai, Dropbox Sign, Docracy, and others) to learn the structure, but always adjust them to your own work and country.

---

## Step 5: Send, Discuss, Sign

1. Send the draft and tell the client to read it properly.
2. Be open to reasonable edits. Negotiation is normal.
3. Get the signature **before** you start any work.
4. Save a signed copy for yourself, and give one to the client.

Tools like Dropbox Sign, DocuSign, Zoho Sign, or even a simple signed PDF over email work well.

---

## Step 6: Follow the Contract During the Project

A contract only works if you actually use it.

- Send invoices on time as promised.
- Keep communication in writing (email), so there's a record.
- If the scope changes, update it in writing.
- Don't hand over final files until the final payment is received.

---

## Common Beginner Mistakes to Avoid

- Starting work on just a WhatsApp message or "okay, go ahead"
- Vague scope like "make it good" or "as needed"
- No advance payment
- No limit on revisions
- Not mentioning deadlines or client delays
- Forgetting to say who owns the work
- Being shy to ask for a signature

---

## Quick Checklist

- [ ] Names and details of both parties
- [ ] Clear scope (what's included and excluded)
- [ ] Deliverables and dates
- [ ] Price, advance, due dates, late fees
- [ ] Revision limit
- [ ] Change request process
- [ ] Client responsibilities
- [ ] Ownership and portfolio rights
- [ ] Confidentiality
- [ ] Termination terms
- [ ] Liability and governing law
- [ ] Signatures from both sides

---

## Coaching Advice

Treat the contract as part of your professional image. Clients take you more seriously when you send a clear agreement, and the good ones appreciate it. The ones who push back hard on a fair contract are often the ones who would have caused trouble later.

Important info: on your early stages of your career in freelance just an formal invoice is enough no need of a complex agreement. This can be applicable to the budget under $1000 dollars
